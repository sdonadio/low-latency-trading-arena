Build an HFT trading system: everything, end to end

A price changes at the exchange. A few hundred nanoseconds to a few hundred microseconds later, an order arrives back at the same exchange. Everything that happens in between is a trading system, and you have now built every piece of it. This page puts the pieces on one screen, shows where each nanosecond goes, and then goes one step past the course: kernel bypass and logic in an FPGA.

  • Review hot-path C++ the way a latency engineer would: allocation, exceptions, dispatch, branches, logging.
  • Lay threads out on cores and NUMA nodes, and say what the kernel costs on every packet.

1The whole system on one screen

Why this part exists

Each topic of the course optimized one box. A trading system is the chain of boxes, and the chain is only as fast as the sum of its stages and as reliable as its slowest moment. Before tuning anything, you need the whole picture and a budget for each stage.

Follow one market-data packet clockwise. It leaves the exchange, crosses a short cable, enters your network card, is decoded by the feed handler, updates the book, is handed to the strategy, passes pre-trade risk, becomes an order in the gateway, and leaves through the card again. Tick to trade is the time between the two taps on the wire.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
Why is tick to trade measured between two taps on the wire rather than inside the program?
The wire is what the exchange sees. A clock inside the program misses the time the packet spends in the network card and the kernel on the way in and out, which on a kernel path is the largest part of the budget.
In the starter configuration, which three segments are the largest, and what single topic fixes each?
Receive and send through the kernel (kernel bypass), text decoding and encoding (binary wire formats and a hand-written codec), and the thread hand-off through a mutex queue (a lock-free SPSC ring).
Why does removing the shared-core choice change p99 much more than the median?
Preemption by another task is rare, so it barely moves the median, but when it happens it costs tens of microseconds. Rare and large is exactly what p99 measures.
Part 2 · how to read every number on this page

2Latency is a distribution, not a number

Why this part exists

"Our tick to trade is 2 microseconds" is not a statement anyone in the industry accepts. Two microseconds at which percentile, measured where, with which clock? The money is made or lost in the moments when the market is busiest, and those are exactly the moments that live in the tail.

Run the same path a million times and you get a histogram, not a value. Most runs bunch around the median. A few are much slower: an interrupt landed on your core, a cache line was stolen, the allocator went to the operating system. Those few decide whether you trade on the moves that matter. You studied the tail in Profiling, the tail and production and computed percentiles online in Algorithmic complexity and time series.

Your p50 improved from 2.1 µs to 1.9 µs but p99 went from 9 µs to 14 µs. Did the change help?
Probably not. The busy moments, where opportunities are, now cost 5 µs more. A change that trades median for tail is usually a regression in trading.
Why is the mean a poor summary of latency?
The distribution is skewed with a long right tail. A few huge samples pull the mean around, while a 1% hiccup hardly moves it. It describes neither the typical tick nor the bad ones.
Why do firms measure on a tap rather than with clock reads inside the program?
The tap sees what the exchange sees, including the network card and the kernel, with one hardware clock for both directions and no cost on the hot path. In-process clocks miss those stages and each read costs tens of nanoseconds.
What is the chance that at least one of 100 ticks lands beyond your p99?
1 - 0.99^100, about 63%. In a burst, the tail is the norm.
Part 3 · the paradox of the fast path

3Keep the hot path hot

Why this part exists

The code that sends an order is the most important code you own, and it almost never runs. Between two decisions, millions of instructions of feed handling and bookkeeping run on the same core and push the strategy's code and data out of the caches. When the moment finally comes, the fast path is cold.

Caches and branch predictors remember what ran recently, not what matters. A path that runs a few times per second is, from the CPU's point of view, cold code. The fix used across the industry sounds odd at first: run the whole decision path all the time, with a dummy order that is built, checked and encoded exactly like a real one, and then thrown away at the very last step. You met the caches and the 64-byte line in Memory management and smart pointers and the branch predictor in SIMD and kernel bypass.

What to notice

The cost of a decision depends less on its own code than on how long ago it last ran. Warming makes the decision cost almost independent of the firing rate, at the price of burning CPU on orders that never leave. It spends throughput to buy latency, which is the trade you will see on every part of this page.

Remember

A rarely executed path is a cold path. Keep it hot by exercising the exact same code and data continuously, and make the discard step the very last one before the wire.

In your bot: if your bot trades rarely, time its first order after a quiet period, then time it again after adding a warm-up call every few ticks.

What a warm-up looks like

void on_tick(const Update& u) {
  book.apply(u);
  if (auto s = strategy.evaluate(book)) {
    send_live(build_order(*s));            // the real thing
  } else if (++since_warm == kWarmEvery) {
    since_warm = 0;
    Order o = build_order(strategy.dummy()); // same code path
    risk.check(o);                         // same tables touched
    gateway.encode(o, frame);              // same template
    nic.stage(frame, Commit::Discard);     // last step differs
  }
}

Rules for warming safely

  • Exercise the same functions and the same data: a separate "warm" function warms the wrong code.
  • Make the difference between a dummy and a real order a single flag at the very end, ideally enforced by the card or by hardware that drops it.
  • Test the discard path as hard as the send path: a dummy that reaches the exchange is a real order.
  • Warm the whole chain: signal, risk, encode, and the send preparation.
  • Count the cost: warming uses a core's cycles and memory bandwidth that the feed handler might want.
Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
Your strategy's code is only 3 KB, far less than the 48 KB L1. Why can it still be cold when it fires?
Size is not the issue, recency is. Everything else that ran since the last decision competed for the same cache sets and evicted it, and the branch predictor was retrained by other code.
Why must the dummy order go through the real functions rather than a lighter copy?
Caches hold specific addresses. A lighter copy warms different code and different data, so the real path stays cold.
What is the safety risk of warming, and how is it contained?
A dummy order could be sent for real. Keep the difference to one flag at the last step, have the card or hardware enforce the discard, and test it.
Part 4 · the physics under every line of code

4The memory hierarchy and the sizes of things

Why this part exists

On a modern core, arithmetic is almost free and waiting for memory is almost everything. If you know a handful of latencies and a handful of sizes by heart, you can predict which design is fast before writing it, and you can read a profile without guessing.

Memory is a ladder: tiny and fast near the core, huge and slow far from it. Data moves between rungs in 64-byte cache lines, never in single bytes. Two consequences shape all low-latency code: keep the data you need together and small, and never let two cores fight over the same line. You built the flat book in Data structures for HFT, the pools in Custom allocators, and padded the ring indices in Lock-free pipelines.

What to notice

The tree is not slow because of its O(log n). It is slow because every step is a dependent load: the CPU cannot start fetching node k+1 until node k has arrived, so the misses add up one after another. The flat array turns the same walk into a stream.

Remember

Contiguous beats linked. Prefer flat arrays indexed by price offset, sorted vectors, and open-addressing tables over trees and lists on the hot path.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
A DRAM miss costs about 80 ns. Roughly how many dependent misses fit in a 1 µs tick-to-trade budget?
About twelve, and that is with nothing else to do. That is why a pointer-chasing structure on the hot path can consume the whole budget by itself.
Why can the prefetcher help the flat array but not the tree?
The flat array's next address is predictable from the current one (the next line). In a tree, the next address is stored inside the node being loaded, so it is unknown until the miss completes.
Two threads write different variables and never read each other's. Can they still slow each other down?
Yes, if the variables share a cache line. Each write needs exclusive ownership of the whole line, so the line ping-pongs between cores. That is false sharing.

5C++ on the hot path: a code review

Why this part exists

Most hot-path slowness is not an algorithm problem. It is a handful of ordinary C++ habits, each harmless in normal code, that cost tens to thousands of nanoseconds when they sit between a tick and an order. Recognising them on sight is the skill.

Remember

On the hot path: no allocation, no exceptions thrown, no system calls, no formatting, no locks, no unpredictable branches you can avoid, no shared ownership, and resolve as much as possible at compile time.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
Exceptions are often called "zero cost". Why are they still banned on the hot path?
They are close to zero cost only when nothing is thrown. A throw walks unwind tables and can cost microseconds, and the rare reject that throws is exactly the tail you are graded on. Return a status instead.
Why does replacing a virtual call with CRTP or a template parameter help more than the few nanoseconds of the call itself?
The compiler can see the target, so it inlines it and optimizes across the boundary: constants propagate, branches fold, loads are hoisted. The indirect call hides all of that.
How do you keep logging without paying for it on the hot path?
Write a small fixed binary record (ids, numbers, a timestamp) into a pre-allocated lock-free ring and let another thread on another core format and write it.
Part 6 · concurrency is a cost you choose to pay

6Threads and cores: one job per core, nothing shared

Why this part exists

More threads do not make one tick faster. They add hand-offs, shared cache lines and scheduling, and each of those adds latency or jitter. A low-latency system uses threads only to keep slow work away from the fast path, then gives each fast thread a core of its own that nobody else touches.

The rules are few. Pin each hot thread to one core. Isolate those cores from the scheduler (the isolcpus and nohz_full boot options) and steer interrupts elsewhere. Pass data between threads through a single-producer single-consumer ring, not a lock. Keep the threads, their memory and the network card on the same NUMA node. And spin instead of sleeping. You built the atomics in Atomics and memory models and the ring in Lock-free pipelines.

Remember

One hot thread per isolated core, interrupts elsewhere, SPSC rings between them, everything on the NIC's NUMA node, and spin rather than sleep on the hot path.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
Why does isolating a core with isolcpus not stop every interruption?
It keeps the scheduler from placing other tasks there, but hardware interrupts and some kernel work still land unless you also steer interrupt affinity and use options such as nohz_full.
Your feed thread runs on node 0, next to the card, and your strategy thread on node 1. What does every tick pay?
The ring between them lives in one node's memory, so the other side reads it across the socket interconnect: a remote access on every hand-off, typically a few hundred nanoseconds.
Why spin instead of sleeping on a condition variable?
Waking a sleeping thread goes through the scheduler and costs microseconds. A spinning thread notices new data in tens of nanoseconds, at the price of a core at 100% all day.
Part 7 · the largest line item in a software system

7The network and the kernel

Why this part exists

With tuned C++, your own code is a few hundred nanoseconds. The kernel's receive and send paths are several microseconds. The operating system is built to share a machine fairly among many programs; a trading system wants the opposite, one program owning the card and the core. So the fast path goes around the kernel.

On the normal path, the card writes the packet into memory the kernel owns, raises an interrupt, and the kernel runs its driver, IP and UDP code, queues the data on a socket and wakes your thread, which copies it out. Busy polling removes the interrupt and the wake-up. Kernel bypass maps the card's rings into your process, so the packet lands in your memory and your loop finds it with a plain load. On-card logic acts on the bytes before they even reach memory. You met epoll and busy polling in Async I/O and serialization, the packet path in SIMD and kernel bypass, and the stack itself in The wire and the machine.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
What exactly does kernel bypass remove from the receive path?
The interrupt, the kernel's driver and protocol processing, the socket queue, the thread wake-up and the copy into user memory. The card writes directly into memory your process owns, and your loop polls the ring.
Why does busy polling cost a full core even when the market is quiet?
The thread never sleeps; it re-reads the ring or the socket in a tight loop so that it notices a packet within nanoseconds. Idle time is spent spinning.
What do you give up when you bypass the kernel?
Its services: the TCP/IP stack (you need a user-space stack or a simpler protocol), sharing the card with other programs, standard tools that watch sockets, and some safety. You also own buffer management and recovery.
Why would an exchange make every cable in the colocation hall the same length?
So that no customer gains nanoseconds from the position of its rack. With cable at about 5 ns per metre, a 50 m difference would be a 250 ns head start.
Part 8 · beyond the CPU

8FPGA: tick to trade in hardware

Why this part exists

After kernel bypass and perfect C++, a software system still spends hundreds of nanoseconds crossing PCIe, waiting on memory and executing instructions one after another. The only way to go further is to stop running instructions: build the decision as a circuit. That is what a field-programmable gate array lets a firm do, and it changes the economics of the whole system.

An FPGA is a chip of configurable logic blocks, memories and wires that you program into a circuit, written in a hardware description language and compiled for hours. It sits on the network card, between the cable and the host. The trading logic becomes a pipeline: every clock cycle, every stage does its small piece of work on a different message, and each message takes the same number of cycles every time. Software still does everything that is complex, rare or changes often, and it arms the hardware with prices and thresholds in advance; the hardware only fires.

What to notice

Raising the clock adds pipeline stages, because each stage can only do the work that fits in one period minus the register overhead. Latency in nanoseconds barely changes; throughput goes up. And the latency is identical for every message: no caches, no interrupts, no branch predictor, no tail.

Remember

FPGA latency = fixed transceiver and MAC time + pipeline cycles × clock period. Parse while the bytes arrive (cut-through), never wait for the whole frame. Deterministic latency is as valuable as low latency.

What to notice

The winning design is a split, not a choice. The fast path is small, stable and simple enough for a circuit; the strategy's brain changes daily and needs software. One function left in software on the fast path costs two PCIe crossings, which is most of the advantage.

Remember

Hybrid design: software arms, hardware fires. Put parsing, the traded symbols' book, a simple trigger, risk checks and order encoding in the FPGA; keep models, full books, recovery, sessions and reporting in software.

Why does a faster FPGA clock not reduce latency much?
The amount of logic per message is fixed. At a faster clock each stage can do less work, so the pipeline needs more stages. Latency stays roughly the same while throughput rises.
What is cut-through processing and why does it matter?
Starting to parse as soon as the needed bytes arrive, without waiting for the end of the frame. At 10 Gb/s every byte takes 0.8 ns, so waiting for a 512-byte frame would add hundreds of nanoseconds.
Name three things that should stay in software and why.
The strategy model and its parameter fitting (changes daily), recovery and snapshots (complex, rare), the exchange session logic and reporting (large, not latency critical). Each would cost huge chip area and weeks of hardware work for no speed benefit.
What is the main cost of an FPGA design besides the hardware?
Development and change time: hardware description languages, long builds, timing closure and verification turn a one-line software change into days of work.
Part 9 · speed without control is a liability

9Risk and safety in a fast system

Why this part exists

A fast system makes mistakes fast. A bug that resends an order on every tick can send tens of thousands of orders before a human notices anything. Pre-trade checks are the only thing between a bug and the market, so they sit on the hot path, they are cheap by design, and nobody is allowed to skip them for speed.

Every order passes a short list of checks before it leaves: size, price collar around a trusted reference, position and loss limits, a message throttle, a stale-data guard, and a kill switch that stops everything and cancels what is resting. Each one is a compare against a precomputed number, a few nanoseconds in software and a single clock cycle in hardware. You met the kill switch and production safety in Profiling, the tail and production and hard limits in Latency arbitrage and the tournament.

What to notice

No single check covers every failure: the throttle limits rate, the position limit limits damage, the collar stops fat fingers, the stale guard stops trading blind, and the kill switch is the last resort. Their total cost is a few tens of nanoseconds in software and a couple of cycles in hardware; moving them outside your system costs microseconds and control.

Remember

Check before the send, not after. Precompute every limit, make each check a compare, keep a kill switch that also cancels resting orders, and never trade on data you cannot prove is fresh.

In your bot: add a max position, a max order size and a message counter per second to your bot's send function, and a flag that stops sending when any of them trips.
Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer
Why must the checks run before the order is sent rather than on the execution reports?
Once the order is out, it can trade. A check on the execution report only tells you how much damage already happened.
A throttle of 20 messages per millisecond is on, and a bug resends orders every tick. Is the firm safe?
No. The bug is slowed to the throttle rate but keeps adding position. A position or loss limit is what stops the damage, and the kill switch stops the bug.
What does a stale-data guard compare, and why does a fast system need one?
The age of the book (or the time since the last sequence number) against a small limit. A fast strategy trading on a frozen feed quotes prices the market has already left, and is picked off.
Why do firms put the same checks in the FPGA when the fast path is in hardware?
Every order must pass the checks, and leaving the chip to check in software would cost the PCIe round trip. In hardware each check is a comparator working in parallel, a cycle or two in total.
