The wire and the machine

Your strategy is fast. Now it has to talk to an exchange. Tonight we follow one order from your code to the matching engine, and one price from the exchange back to you. 0Where the network sits in finance

Why this part exists

Before sockets and headers, see which machines talk and what they say. Every later part of this page zooms into one of these links.

A trading firm and an exchange are two groups of computers. Two links matter most. Order entry goes out from your order gateway to the exchange, and executions come back on it. Market data comes in from the exchange to your feed handler. Around them sit the risk check, the drop copy and the broker. Click a link to see the messages that flow on it.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Why does an exchange not send market data over TCP to every client?
TCP is one connection per client, so the exchange would send the same bytes thousands of times and the last client would get them last. Multicast sends once and the network copies the packet to every subscriber at the same time. TCP also stalls every byte behind one lost packet.

  
What is a drop copy, and why is it not the same link as order entry?
A copy of every execution, sent to a second system (risk desk, back office). It is separate so that someone outside the trading path can see fills even if the trading system is broken or misbehaving.

  
Which link in the map involves no network at all?
Inside the firm: feed handler to strategy to risk check to gateway, usually on one machine through shared memory or other IPC. That is Part 1.







  
Part 1

  
1A socket is one kind of IPC

  
Why this part exists
You already moved data between processes with shared memory.
  A socket is the same job, done by the kernel, and it can also cross machines. Knowing the cost of each choice tells you where a socket belongs.

  
IPC means inter-process communication: two programs exchanging bytes. On one machine you can use
  shared memory, a pipe or a Unix domain socket. Across machines you need a
  network socket: TCP or UDP. The big differences are who copies the bytes and how many times the kernel gets involved.

Same machine and you own both ends: shared memory. Different machines: a socket. The latency is mostly software (kernel, copies), not distance.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
A pipe and a Unix domain socket both stay on one machine. Why are they still microseconds and not nanoseconds?
Each message needs a system call to write and one to read, and the kernel copies the bytes into its own buffer and out again. The mode switches and copies are the cost.

  
Why can you not use shared memory to talk to the exchange?
Shared memory only works between processes on the same machine (they map the same physical pages). The exchange runs on other machines, so the bytes have to cross a network.

  
Which of these mechanisms keep message boundaries?
UDP sockets (and Unix datagram sockets) keep them: one send is one receive. Pipes, TCP and stream Unix sockets are byte streams, so you must frame messages yourself. Shared memory keeps whatever slots you design.






  
Part 2 · the map for the rest of the page

  
2The network is organized in layers

  
Why this part exists
Each problem on the wire belongs to one layer: a dirty fiber is layer 1,
  a lost packet is layer 3 or 4, a rejected logon is layer 5. When you know the layer, you know where to look and who to call.

  
The OSI model splits networking into seven layers. Each layer does one job and talks only to the layer
  above and below. The internet itself uses a simpler TCP/IP model with four layers; the two line up as shown.
  On the way out, every layer adds its own header around your message. On the way in, every layer removes one.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Which two OSI layers have no separate layer in the TCP/IP model, and where do they go?
Presentation (6) and session (5). TCP/IP puts them in its application layer, together with layer 7. In practice your program (or a library like a FIX engine) does all three.

  
On a 40-byte binary order sent over TCP, roughly how many bytes cross the wire, and what share is headers?
About 129 byte times: 8 + 3 bytes of encoding and session framing, 20 TCP, 20 IP, 18 Ethernet, and 20 of preamble and gap on the wire. Headers are about 69 % of it.

  
A FIX logon is rejected because the sequence number is wrong. Which layer is that?
Session (layer 5). The cable, the IP route and the TCP connection all worked; the conversation itself is out of step.






  
Part 3 · OSI layer 1

  
3The physical layer: light, air and copper

  
Why this part exists
No software can beat the speed of light. The physical path sets the
  floor of your latency, and in finance firms pay real money to lower that floor.

  
Inside a data center, your server reaches the exchange through a cross-connect: a fiber
  or copper cable run between your rack and the exchange's. Between two data centers, the data travels over
  long-haul fiber or over microwave and millimeter-wave radio from tower to tower. Light in glass moves at
  about two thirds of its speed in air, so a straight radio path can be milliseconds faster than a fiber that follows roads and railways.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Why is light slower in fiber than in air?
Glass has a refractive index of about 1.47, so light moves at about c / 1.47, roughly two thirds of c. In air the index is about 1.0003.

  
Over 1,200 km, a fiber route is 30 % longer than the straight line. About how much slower is it than a straight radio path?
Fiber: 1,560 km at 4.9 µs/km is about 7.6 ms. Radio: about 1,224 km at 3.3 µs/km, about 4.1 ms. The radio path wins by about 3.5 ms one-way.

  
Why do firms still keep a fiber link next to a microwave link?
Microwave has less bandwidth and fades in heavy rain. Fiber is slower but reliable, so it carries the traffic when the radio link drops.







  
Part 4 · OSI layer 2

  
4The data link layer: Ethernet frames and switches

  
Why this part exists
Every packet you send is carried inside an Ethernet frame, and every frame
  goes through at least one switch. The switch's design adds from nanoseconds to microseconds to every message.

  
Layer 2 moves a frame from one network card to the next on the same network. The frame starts with the
  destination and source MAC addresses (48-bit hardware addresses) and ends with a checksum, the FCS. A switch
  reads the destination MAC and sends the frame out of the right port. A VLAN tag (4 bytes) splits one physical network into
  separate virtual ones, for example market data on one VLAN and order entry on another.

What to notice
A store-and-forward switch waits for the whole frame before sending it on, so big frames and slow links add up at every hop. A cut-through switch starts sending once it has read the destination MAC. A layer-1 switch does not read the frame at all: it copies the signal.
Remember
Serialization time = frame bits / link speed. At 10 Gb/s, 1,500 bytes take 1.2 µs to put on the wire. Low-latency networks use cut-through switches, small frames and fast links.
In your bot: your orders are small frames, so serialization is tiny; the number of hops and the switch type matter more.


Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
What is the destination MAC address of a frame carrying a packet to multicast group 239.255.0.1?
01:00:5e:7f:00:01. Multicast MACs start with 01:00:5e and carry the low 23 bits of the group address, so the switch can recognize group traffic.

  
At 10 Gb/s, how long does a 1,538-byte frame (with preamble and gap) take to serialize?
1,538 × 8 bits / 10 Gb/s, about 1.23 µs. A store-and-forward switch pays it again at every hop.

  
Why put market data and order entry on separate VLANs?
To keep the heavy, bursty multicast traffic from competing with order traffic, and to control which ports can see which traffic, without buying separate switches.







  
Part 5 · OSI layer 3

  
5The network layer: IP addresses, routing and multicast

  
Why this part exists
Layer 2 stops at the edge of one network. IP is what carries your order from
  your data center to the exchange's, and IP multicast is how one market data packet reaches every subscriber at once.

  
Every machine has an IP address (IPv4: four numbers, like 10.1.0.15). Routers read the destination
  address and forward the packet one hop closer. Each router lowers the TTL (time to live) by one; at zero the packet is dropped,
  so a routing loop cannot keep it alive forever. Addresses from 224.0.0.0 to 239.255.255.255 are multicast groups:
  receivers join a group, and the network copies each packet to all of them.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Who decides which machines receive a multicast packet?
The receivers. Each one joins the group (IP_ADD_MEMBERSHIP, which sends an IGMP join), and the switches copy the group's packets to the ports that joined. The sender only knows the group address.

  
Why does every router lower the TTL?
So that a packet caught in a routing loop dies after a fixed number of hops instead of circling forever. A small TTL also keeps multicast inside the local network.

  
What does IP not promise you?
Delivery, ordering and no duplicates. Packets can be lost, reordered or duplicated. TCP adds those guarantees; with UDP you check sequence numbers yourself.






  
Part 6 · OSI layer 4

  
6The transport layer: TCP and UDP

  
Why this part exists
This is the layer your code chooses when it calls socket().
  TCP and UDP each leave you one job to do yourself, and the lab is about doing those two jobs right.

  
TCP opens a connection, numbers every byte, resends what is lost and delivers everything in order. The price:
  a handshake, a stall when a packet is lost, and a byte stream with no message boundaries, so you must frame messages.
  UDP sends independent datagrams: no connection, no resend, no ordering, but one send is exactly one receive and it can go to a
  multicast group. The price: you must detect loss yourself, with sequence numbers. Exchanges use TCP for order entry and
  UDP multicast for market data.

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Your client reads 7 bytes from a TCP socket: "100.03\n". Can you assume the next recv() starts with a new price?
No. TCP is a byte stream. The next recv() can return half a price, or several prices. Always append to a buffer, cut out complete messages, and keep the remainder.

  
Why is UDP multicast preferred for market data even though it can lose packets?
One send reaches every subscriber at the same time, and a lost packet does not stall the ones behind it. With TCP, one lost segment holds back every later byte for a round trip (head-of-line blocking). Loss is handled with sequence numbers, A/B feeds and snapshots.

  
Why is order entry on TCP and not UDP?
An order must never be lost or duplicated and must stay in order with its cancels. TCP guarantees that, and there is only one receiver, so multicast brings nothing.

  
What does TCP_NODELAY turn off, and why do trading systems set it?
Nagle's algorithm, which holds small writes until earlier data is acknowledged. Holding an order for a round trip costs far more than the extra packet.






  
Part 7 · hands-on

  
7Lab: a moving average over TCP, then over UDP multicast

  
Why this part exists
You only believe framing and gap detection once you have seen your own
  client survive a split message and a lost packet. This lab is small on purpose: four short programs, every line visible.

  
A server sends prices. Your client computes a running N-point simple moving average (SMA) and prints it.
  You do it twice. (a) TCP: one price per line, the client frames the byte stream on '\n'.
  (b) UDP multicast: one 16-byte datagram per price (sequence number + price in cents) to group 239.255.0.1; every client
  that joined gets a copy and checks the sequence. Both servers use the same seeded random walk, so the two clients print the same averages until a packet is lost.
  The code is in your starter repo under labs/network_sockets/.

// common.hpp: shared by the four Warm-up programs.
// Header-only, POSIX + the C++20 standard library, nothing else.
#pragma once

#include <cstddef>
#include <cstdint>
#include <random>
#include <string_view>
#include <vector>

namespace s8 {

// "--name value" on the command line, or the fallback when the flag is absent.
inline const char* arg(int argc, char** argv, std::string_view name, const char* fallback) {
  for (int i = 1; i + 1 < argc; ++i)
    if (name == argv[i]) return argv[i + 1];
  return fallback;
}

// A deterministic random walk in integer cents, so both servers send the same prices
// on every machine. std::mt19937's output is fixed by the standard; the distributions
// are not, which is why we take a plain modulo instead of uniform_int_distribution.
class PriceWalk {
 public:
  explicit PriceWalk(std::uint32_t seed = 42, std::int64_t start_cents = 10000)
      : rng_(seed), cents_(start_cents) {}
  std::int64_t next() {
    cents_ += static_cast<std::int64_t>(rng_() % 11) - 5;  // move -5 .. +5 cents
    if (cents_ < 1) cents_ = 1;
    return cents_;
  }

 private:
  std::mt19937 rng_;
  std::int64_t cents_;
};

// N-point simple moving average in O(1) per update: a ring of the last N prices and a
// running sum. Add the new price, subtract the one that falls out of the window.
class MovingAverage {
 public:
  explicit MovingAverage(std::size_t n) : ring_(n, 0.0) {}
  double push(double x) {
    if (count_ == ring_.size()) sum_ -= ring_[head_];  // oldest leaves the window
    else ++count_;
    ring_[head_] = x;
    sum_ += x;
    head_ = (head_ + 1) % ring_.size();
    return sum_ / static_cast<double>(count_);
  }
  bool full() const { return count_ == ring_.size(); }
  std::size_t count() const { return count_; }

 private:
  std::vector<double> ring_;
  std::size_t head_ = 0, count_ = 0;
  double sum_ = 0.0;
};

// The UDP packet: 16 bytes, both fields big-endian (network byte order).
//   bytes 0..7   sequence number: starts at 1, +1 per packet, dropped packets included
//   bytes 8..15  price in integer cents
inline constexpr std::size_t kPacketSize = 16;

inline void put_be64(unsigned char* p, std::uint64_t v) {
  for (int i = 7; i >= 0; --i) { p[i] = static_cast<unsigned char>(v & 0xff); v >>= 8; }
}
inline std::uint64_t get_be64(const unsigned char* p) {
  std::uint64_t v = 0;
  for (int i = 0; i < 8; ++i) v = (v << 8) | p[i];
  return v;
}

}  // namespace s8



// tcp_price_server.cpp: accept one client at a time and send it a stream of prices.
// Framing: one price per line, "100.12\n". The README explains newline vs length prefix.
//
//   ./tcp_price_server [--port 5555] [--count 50] [--interval-ms 100] [--split 1]
//
// --split 1 sends every line in two pieces, 20 ms apart, to show that TCP does not
// keep your message boundaries: the client must frame the byte stream itself.
#include "common.hpp"

#include <arpa/inet.h>
#include <netinet/in.h>
#include <netinet/tcp.h>
#include <sys/socket.h>
#include <unistd.h>

#include <cerrno>
#include <chrono>
#include <csignal>
#include <cstdio>
#include <cstdlib>
#include <thread>

// send() may write fewer bytes than asked: loop until the whole buffer is out.
static bool send_all(int fd, const char* p, std::size_t n) {
  while (n > 0) {
    ssize_t k = ::send(fd, p, n, 0);
    if (k < 0 && errno == EINTR) continue;
    if (k <= 0) return false;  // the client went away (EPIPE, ECONNRESET)
    p += k;
    n -= static_cast<std::size_t>(k);
  }
  return true;
}

int main(int argc, char** argv) {
  const int port = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--port", "5555"));
  const int count = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--count", "50"));
  const int interval_ms = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--interval-ms", "100"));
  const bool split = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--split", "0")) != 0;
  std::signal(SIGPIPE, SIG_IGN);  // a vanished client must give EPIPE, not kill the server

  int ls = ::socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0);
  if (ls < 0) { std::perror("socket"); return 1; }
  int one = 1;
  // Restart right away on the same port instead of waiting out TIME_WAIT.
  ::setsockopt(ls, SOL_SOCKET, SO_REUSEADDR, &one, sizeof one);

  sockaddr_in addr{};
  addr.sin_family = AF_INET;
  addr.sin_addr.s_addr = htonl(INADDR_ANY);
  addr.sin_port = htons(static_cast<std::uint16_t>(port));
  if (::bind(ls, reinterpret_cast<sockaddr*>(&addr), sizeof addr) < 0) { std::perror("bind"); return 1; }
  if (::listen(ls, 8) < 0) { std::perror("listen"); return 1; }
  std::printf("[server] listening on port %d: %d prices per client, one every %d ms%s\n",
              port, count, interval_ms, split ? ", each line split in two sends" : "");
  std::fflush(stdout);

  for (;;) {
    sockaddr_in peer{};
    socklen_t plen = sizeof peer;
    int c = ::accept(ls, reinterpret_cast<sockaddr*>(&peer), &plen);  // a NEW socket per client
    if (c < 0) {
      if (errno == EINTR) continue;
      std::perror("accept");
      return 1;
    }
    char ip[INET_ADDRSTRLEN];
    ::inet_ntop(AF_INET, &peer.sin_addr, ip, sizeof ip);
    std::printf("[server] client %s:%d connected\n", ip, ntohs(peer.sin_port));
    std::fflush(stdout);

    // Small messages: send each one now instead of letting Nagle wait to batch them.
    ::setsockopt(c, IPPROTO_TCP, TCP_NODELAY, &one, sizeof one);

    s8::PriceWalk walk;  // same seed: every client gets the same stream
    int sent = 0;
    bool ok = true;
    for (; sent < count && ok; ++sent) {
      const std::int64_t cents = walk.next();
      char line[32];
      const int n = std::snprintf(line, sizeof line, "%lld.%02lld\n",
                                  static_cast<long long>(cents / 100), static_cast<long long>(cents % 100));
      if (split) {
        const int half = n / 2;
        ok = send_all(c, line, static_cast<std::size_t>(half));
        std::this_thread::sleep_for(std::chrono::milliseconds(20));
        ok = ok && send_all(c, line + half, static_cast<std::size_t>(n - half));
      } else {
        ok = send_all(c, line, static_cast<std::size_t>(n));
      }
      if (ok) std::this_thread::sleep_for(std::chrono::milliseconds(interval_ms));
    }
    if (!ok) --sent;  // the last line did not make it out
    std::printf("[server] %s after %d prices; waiting for the next client\n",
                ok ? "done" : "client disconnected", sent);
    std::fflush(stdout);
    ::close(c);
  }
}



// tcp_ma_client.cpp: connect, frame the byte stream into lines, print an N-point SMA.
//
//   ./tcp_ma_client [--host 127.0.0.1] [--port 5555] [--window 5] [--show-recv 1]
//
// --show-recv 1 prints the size of every recv(), so you can see that one recv() is
// not one message.
#include "common.hpp"

#include <arpa/inet.h>
#include <netinet/in.h>
#include <sys/socket.h>
#include <unistd.h>

#include <cerrno>
#include <cstdio>
#include <cstdlib>
#include <string>

int main(int argc, char** argv) {
  const char* host = s8::arg(argc, argv, "--host", "127.0.0.1");
  const int port = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--port", "5555"));
  const int window = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--window", "5"));
  const bool show_recv = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--show-recv", "0")) != 0;
  if (window < 1) { std::fprintf(stderr, "--window must be at least 1\n"); return 1; }

  int fd = ::socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0);
  if (fd < 0) { std::perror("socket"); return 1; }
  sockaddr_in addr{};
  addr.sin_family = AF_INET;
  addr.sin_port = htons(static_cast<std::uint16_t>(port));
  if (::inet_pton(AF_INET, host, &addr.sin_addr) != 1) {
    std::fprintf(stderr, "--host must be a dotted IPv4 address, e.g. 127.0.0.1\n");
    return 1;
  }
  if (::connect(fd, reinterpret_cast<sockaddr*>(&addr), sizeof addr) < 0) {  // SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK
    std::perror("connect");
    std::fprintf(stderr, "is tcp_price_server running on %s:%d?\n", host, port);
    return 1;
  }
  std::printf("[client] connected to %s:%d, window N=%d\n", host, port, window);

  s8::MovingAverage ma(static_cast<std::size_t>(window));
  std::string buf;  // bytes received but not yet framed into a full line
  char tmp[4096];
  long msgs = 0;
  double last = 0.0;
  for (;;) {
    ssize_t n = ::recv(fd, tmp, sizeof tmp, 0);
    if (n < 0 && errno == EINTR) continue;
    if (n < 0) { std::perror("recv"); break; }
    if (n == 0) { std::printf("[client] server closed the connection\n"); break; }
    if (show_recv) std::printf("[client] recv() returned %zd bytes\n", n);
    buf.append(tmp, static_cast<std::size_t>(n));

    // Frame: every complete line is one price. Keep the unfinished tail for next time.
    std::size_t start = 0, nl;
    while ((nl = buf.find('\n', start)) != std::string::npos) {
      char* end = nullptr;
      const double px = std::strtod(buf.c_str() + start, &end);  // stops at the '\n'
      const bool good = end == buf.c_str() + nl;
      start = nl + 1;
      if (!good) { std::fprintf(stderr, "[client] bad line skipped\n"); continue; }
      last = ma.push(px);
      ++msgs;
      if (ma.full())
        std::printf("#%-3ld price %7.2f   sma%d %9.4f\n", msgs, px, window, last);
      else
        std::printf("#%-3ld price %7.2f   sma%d warming up (%zu/%d)\n", msgs, px, window, ma.count(), window);
    }
    buf.erase(0, start);
  }
  if (!buf.empty()) std::printf("[client] %zu bytes of an unfinished line left over\n", buf.size());
  std::printf("[client] %ld prices, last sma%d = %.4f\n", msgs, window, last);
  ::close(fd);
  return 0;
}



// udp_price_publisher.cpp: publish prices to a multicast group, one datagram each,
// with a sequence number. Nobody connects to a publisher; it never learns who listens.
//
//   ./udp_price_publisher [--group 239.255.0.1] [--port 30001] [--iface 127.0.0.1]
//                         [--count 50] [--interval-ms 100] [--drop 0.0] [--seed 7]
//
// --drop P skips each send with probability P (0..1) but still uses up its sequence
// number, exactly like a packet lost on the path. --count 0 publishes forever.
#include "common.hpp"

#include <arpa/inet.h>
#include <netinet/in.h>
#include <sys/socket.h>
#include <unistd.h>

#include <chrono>
#include <cstdio>
#include <cstdlib>
#include <random>
#include <thread>

int main(int argc, char** argv) {
  const char* group = s8::arg(argc, argv, "--group", "239.255.0.1");
  const int port = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--port", "30001"));
  const char* iface = s8::arg(argc, argv, "--iface", "127.0.0.1");
  const long count = std::atol(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--count", "50"));
  const int interval_ms = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--interval-ms", "100"));
  const double drop = std::atof(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--drop", "0"));
  const auto seed = static_cast<std::uint32_t>(std::atol(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--seed", "7")));

  int fd = ::socket(AF_INET, SOCK_DGRAM, 0);
  if (fd < 0) { std::perror("socket"); return 1; }

  unsigned char ttl = 1;   // stay on the local network: routers will not forward it
  unsigned char loop = 1;  // also deliver to receivers on this same host
  ::setsockopt(fd, IPPROTO_IP, IP_MULTICAST_TTL, &ttl, sizeof ttl);
  ::setsockopt(fd, IPPROTO_IP, IP_MULTICAST_LOOP, &loop, sizeof loop);
  in_addr out{};
  if (::inet_pton(AF_INET, iface, &out) != 1) { std::fprintf(stderr, "bad --iface\n"); return 1; }
  if (::setsockopt(fd, IPPROTO_IP, IP_MULTICAST_IF, &out, sizeof out) < 0) {  // which NIC sends
    std::perror("IP_MULTICAST_IF");
    return 1;
  }

  sockaddr_in dst{};
  dst.sin_family = AF_INET;
  dst.sin_port = htons(static_cast<std::uint16_t>(port));
  if (::inet_pton(AF_INET, group, &dst.sin_addr) != 1) { std::fprintf(stderr, "bad --group\n"); return 1; }

  std::printf("[pub] group %s:%d via %s, one price every %d ms, drop %.0f%%\n",
              group, port, iface, interval_ms, drop * 100.0);
  std::fflush(stdout);

  s8::PriceWalk walk;  // same prices as the TCP server
  std::mt19937 rng(seed);
  std::uniform_real_distribution<double> u01(0.0, 1.0);
  long dropped = 0;
  for (std::uint64_t seq = 1; count == 0 || seq <= static_cast<std::uint64_t>(count); ++seq) {
    const std::int64_t cents = walk.next();
    const double px = static_cast<double>(cents) / 100.0;
    if (drop > 0.0 && u01(rng) < drop) {
      ++dropped;
      std::printf("[pub] seq %3llu price %7.2f   DROPPED (simulated loss)\n",
                  static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq), px);
    } else {
      unsigned char pkt[s8::kPacketSize];
      s8::put_be64(pkt, seq);
      s8::put_be64(pkt + 8, static_cast<std::uint64_t>(cents));
      const ssize_t n = ::sendto(fd, pkt, sizeof pkt, 0, reinterpret_cast<sockaddr*>(&dst), sizeof dst);
      if (n != static_cast<ssize_t>(sizeof pkt)) { std::perror("sendto"); return 1; }
      std::printf("[pub] seq %3llu price %7.2f\n", static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq), px);
    }
    std::fflush(stdout);
    std::this_thread::sleep_for(std::chrono::milliseconds(interval_ms));
  }
  std::printf("[pub] done, %ld dropped on purpose\n", dropped);
  ::close(fd);
  return 0;
}



// udp_ma_client.cpp: join a multicast group, print an N-point SMA, detect gaps.
//
//   ./udp_ma_client [--group 239.255.0.1] [--port 30001] [--iface 127.0.0.1]
//                   [--window 5] [--idle-ms 3000]
//
// UDP has no "connection closed": the client stops after --idle-ms with no data.
#include "common.hpp"

#include <arpa/inet.h>
#include <netinet/in.h>
#include <sys/socket.h>
#include <sys/time.h>
#include <unistd.h>

#include <cerrno>
#include <cstdio>
#include <cstdlib>

int main(int argc, char** argv) {
  const char* group = s8::arg(argc, argv, "--group", "239.255.0.1");
  const int port = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--port", "30001"));
  const char* iface = s8::arg(argc, argv, "--iface", "127.0.0.1");
  const int window = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--window", "5"));
  const int idle_ms = std::atoi(s8::arg(argc, argv, "--idle-ms", "3000"));
  if (window < 1) { std::fprintf(stderr, "--window must be at least 1\n"); return 1; }

  int fd = ::socket(AF_INET, SOCK_DGRAM, 0);
  if (fd < 0) { std::perror("socket"); return 1; }

  // Several clients on one host must bind the same port. Linux: SO_REUSEADDR is enough
  // for multicast. macOS / BSD: SO_REUSEPORT is also needed. (On Linux, SO_REUSEPORT
  // means load balancing for unicast, which is not what we want here.)
  int one = 1;
  ::setsockopt(fd, SOL_SOCKET, SO_REUSEADDR, &one, sizeof one);
#if defined(SO_REUSEPORT) && !defined(__linux__)
  ::setsockopt(fd, SOL_SOCKET, SO_REUSEPORT, &one, sizeof one);
#endif

  sockaddr_in addr{};
  addr.sin_family = AF_INET;
  addr.sin_addr.s_addr = htonl(INADDR_ANY);
  addr.sin_port = htons(static_cast<std::uint16_t>(port));
  if (::bind(fd, reinterpret_cast<sockaddr*>(&addr), sizeof addr) < 0) { std::perror("bind"); return 1; }

  // Join the group on one interface: the kernel sends an IGMP join, the switch starts
  // copying the group's packets to us. The publisher never finds out.
  ip_mreq m{};
  if (::inet_pton(AF_INET, group, &m.imr_multiaddr) != 1 || ::inet_pton(AF_INET, iface, &m.imr_interface) != 1) {
    std::fprintf(stderr, "bad --group or --iface\n");
    return 1;
  }
  if (::setsockopt(fd, IPPROTO_IP, IP_ADD_MEMBERSHIP, &m, sizeof m) < 0) {
    std::perror("IP_ADD_MEMBERSHIP");
    return 1;
  }

  timeval tv{};
  tv.tv_sec = idle_ms / 1000;
  tv.tv_usec = (idle_ms % 1000) * 1000;
  ::setsockopt(fd, SOL_SOCKET, SO_RCVTIMEO, &tv, sizeof tv);
  std::printf("[client] joined %s:%d on %s, window N=%d\n", group, port, iface, window);
  std::fflush(stdout);

  s8::MovingAverage ma(static_cast<std::size_t>(window));
  std::uint64_t expected = 0;  // 0 = not synced yet: the first packet sets it
  long received = 0, gaps = 0, late = 0;
  unsigned long long missing = 0;
  double last = 0.0;
  for (;;) {
    unsigned char buf[1500];
    const ssize_t n = ::recvfrom(fd, buf, sizeof buf, 0, nullptr, nullptr);  // exactly one datagram
    if (n < 0 && errno == EINTR) continue;
    if (n < 0 && (errno == EAGAIN || errno == EWOULDBLOCK)) {
      std::printf("[client] no data for %d ms: publisher finished or gone\n", idle_ms);
      break;
    }
    if (n < 0) { std::perror("recvfrom"); break; }
    if (n != static_cast<ssize_t>(s8::kPacketSize)) {
      std::printf("[client] ignored a %zd-byte datagram\n", n);
      continue;
    }
    const std::uint64_t seq = s8::get_be64(buf);
    const double px = static_cast<double>(static_cast<std::int64_t>(s8::get_be64(buf + 8))) / 100.0;

    if (expected == 0) {
      std::printf("[client] first packet is seq %llu: synced\n", static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq));
      expected = seq;
    }
    if (seq < expected) {  // late or duplicate (an A/B feed sends everything twice)
      ++late;
      std::printf("[client] seq %llu is late or a duplicate: ignored\n", static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq));
      continue;
    }
    if (seq > expected) {  // the only evidence of loss is the jump
      ++gaps;
      missing += seq - expected;
      std::printf("[client] GAP: expected seq %llu, got %llu (%llu missing)\n",
                  static_cast<unsigned long long>(expected), static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq),
                  static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq - expected));
    }
    expected = seq + 1;
    ++received;
    last = ma.push(px);
    if (ma.full())
      std::printf("seq %3llu price %7.2f   sma%d %9.4f\n", static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq), px, window, last);
    else
      std::printf("seq %3llu price %7.2f   sma%d warming up (%zu/%d)\n",
                  static_cast<unsigned long long>(seq), px, window, ma.count(), window);
    std::fflush(stdout);
  }
  std::printf("[client] received %ld, gaps %ld, missing %llu, late %ld, last sma%d = %.4f\n",
              received, gaps, missing, late, window, last);
  ::setsockopt(fd, IPPROTO_IP, IP_DROP_MEMBERSHIP, &m, sizeof m);
  ::close(fd);
  return 0;
}

Why a newline and not a length prefix? A price as text is short and never contains '\n',
  and you can read the stream with nc 127.0.0.1 5555. A length prefix (say, 2 bytes of length, then the body) is the
  right choice for binary messages, where any byte value can appear, and the reader knows how many bytes to wait for without scanning. Most binary
  exchange protocols use one. Either way the rule is the same: the stream has no boundaries until you add them.

    •

    • 
    
  • Drop packets: ./udp_price_publisher --drop 0.2. Every client reports the same gaps: the loss happens before the copy.
    • 
    
  • Two or three UDP clients with different --window values. One sendto() per price reaches all of them.
    • 
    
  • Join late: run the publisher with --count 0 (forever), then start a client. Its first packet is not seq 1, and it syncs on whatever arrives. Kill the publisher: UDP sends no goodbye, the client stops after 3 s of silence.

Linux and macOS. Both UDP programs use the loopback interface (--iface 127.0.0.1),
  TTL 1 and IP_MULTICAST_LOOP on, so nothing leaves your machine. To run two clients on one port, Linux needs only SO_REUSEADDR
  for multicast; macOS also needs SO_REUSEPORT, which the client sets there. On Linux, if no packet arrives on loopback, run
  sudo ip link set lo multicast on or pass your LAN address as --iface to both sides.


Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Your TCP client works on localhost without any framing loop: one recv(), one price. Why is it still wrong?
On localhost with a slow sender the kernel usually happens to deliver one line per recv(). On a real network, or with a fast sender, lines get cut and merged (try --split 1 and --interval-ms 0). Correct code frames the byte stream every time.

  
The UDP client sees seq 6 then seq 8. What does it print, and what does it do next?
It prints a GAP line (expected 7, got 8, 1 missing), sets the next expected sequence to 9, and keeps computing the SMA with the prices it has.

  
Why does every UDP client report the same gaps?
The publisher drops the packet before it is sent, so there is nothing for the network to copy. A real loss in one switch port would hit only the clients behind it.

  
Why does the UDP client need a receive timeout?
UDP has no connection, so there is no "closed" signal when the publisher stops. Silence is the only clue; the client gives up after --idle-ms.

Remember

Check yourself · click a question to reveal the answer

  
Why can the lab's UDP client keep going after a gap, while a real feed handler must stop trading?
The lab sends absolute prices, so a lost one only removes a point from the average. A real order book feed sends changes (add, cancel, execute); missing one leaves the book wrong until a snapshot replaces it.

  
You log a one-way latency of -12 µs. What does it tell you?
The two clocks are offset by more than the real latency. The number is meaningless; fix the sync (PTP) or measure a round trip on one clock.

  
Why does epoll beat poll with 10,000 mostly idle connections?
poll passes and scans all 10,000 descriptors at every call. epoll keeps the list in the kernel and returns only the ready ones, so the cost follows activity, not size.
