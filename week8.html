Algorithmic Complexity & Time-Series

Big-O tells you how cost grows. It says nothing about the constant, and at HFT scale the constant is the answer. This week you learn to count cache misses instead of instructions, and to compute every statistic your strategy needs in O(1) per tick — because a window recompute is not slow on average, it is a tail spike exactly when the market is busy.

  • Say where the crossover between an O(n) scan and an O(log n) search actually is on your machine, and why it is not where the textbook says.
  • Convert any windowed statistic into an incremental update: running sum, Welford variance, EWMA, and the starter's rolling_counter.hpp.
  • Track a percentile in bounded memory, and know what accuracy you gave up to do it.
  • Compound a tail: turn per-stage p99.9 into an end-to-end number, and align two venues whose clocks disagree.

Part 1

1What complexity means when n is small and memory is slow

Asymptotic notation describes behaviour as n goes to infinity. Your order book has sixty-four levels and your rolling window has two hundred samples. You are permanently in the regime big-O was designed to ignore.

Three ideas do all the work this week. First, count the right operation: at one nanosecond per arithmetic instruction and ninety per DRAM miss, the complexity that matters is measured in memory accesses, not instructions. An algorithm that touches half as much memory and does twice the arithmetic is nearly twice as fast.

Second, amortised is not worst case. std::vector::push_back is O(1) amortised, which means most calls are a store and an increment and one call in n reallocates and copies everything. Averaged over a million pushes that is constant. Sampled at p99.9 — the number you are graded on — it is a reallocation of the whole buffer while a quote you wanted to send waits. reserve() is not an optimisation, it is the removal of an unbounded tail.

Third, the worst case is your grade. A latency distribution is not summarised by its mean. The trade you lose is the one where the slow path fired, and slow paths are correlated with exactly the moments you care about — the busy tick, the volatile open, the message burst.

The one number to internalise. A cache miss to DRAM is roughly ninety times an arithmetic operation. Any time you are choosing between "more maths" and "more memory", the maths is free. This is the same lesson as Week 7's flat book, now stated as a complexity claim.

Part 2

2How does it work? Update, never recompute

A rolling window slides one sample at a time: one in, one out. Every statistic you want over it can be maintained by folding those two events into a few scalars — which turns O(k) per tick into O(1) per tick and, more importantly, turns a variable cost into a fixed one.

The naive version is a loop over the window on every tick. It is correct, it is easy, and its cost is proportional to your window length, so widening the window to get a smoother signal makes the hot path slower in exact proportion. The incremental version keeps a running total and adjusts it by the arriving and departing samples; its cost does not depend on k at all. Welford does the same trick for variance without the catastrophic cancellation you get from subtracting two nearly equal large numbers, and the starter's RollingCounter does it for "how many events in the last window", using the one fact that makes it possible: the clock only moves forward, so an expired event stays expired and is dropped exactly once.

// include/rolling_counter.hpp — amortised O(1) because the clock is monotonic.
// Each event is added once and expired once, so count() never re-examines one.
struct RollingCounter {
    explicit RollingCounter(uint64_t window_ns)
        : window_(window_ns), buf_(1 << 20) {}      // power of two -> mask, not %

    void add(uint64_t ts_ns) {                       // O(1), never allocates
        buf_[tail_ & (buf_.size() - 1)] = ts_ns;
        ++tail_;
    }
    uint64_t count(uint64_t now_ns) {                // amortised O(1)
        uint64_t cutoff = now_ns > window_ ? now_ns - window_ : 0;
        while (head_ < tail_ && buf_[head_ & (buf_.size() - 1)] <= cutoff)
            ++head_;                                 // each event dies once
        return tail_ - head_;                        // live events, O(1)
    }
  private:
    uint64_t window_;
    std::vector<uint64_t> buf_;
    uint64_t head_ = 0, tail_ = 0;                   // indices grow forever
};
Read the boundary. The contract is "events with ts > now − window", so an event sitting exactly on cutoff is out — which is why the loop expires <= cutoff. Getting this one comparison backwards is the most common way rc_mixed fails, and it is off by exactly one sample, which is exactly the kind of error a smoke test will not show you.

Part 3

3Why it is harder than it looks: percentiles, tails, and clocks

Three places where the O(1) discipline is not enough on its own — because the thing you want to measure is a distribution, the thing you are graded on compounds, and the thing you are measuring against has a different clock.

Widget 4 · tracking a percentile without keeping the data

You want p99.9 of your own tick-to-trade latency, continuously, over millions of samples. Exact means storing and sorting all of them. Three approximations trade accuracy for bounded memory — and one of them is unusable for a tail.

methodmemoryns / insertns / querytail error
Why a reservoir cannot see a tail. Uniform reservoir sampling keeps m samples chosen uniformly at random. To have even one sample from the top 0.1 % of the distribution you need m in the thousands, and to estimate that boundary you need dozens of them. The methods that work on tails — histograms with logarithmic buckets, or a t-digest that deliberately keeps its centroids fine near the extremes and coarse in the middle — are the ones that spend their memory where the question is.

The rule that saves you. Never forward-fill into the future relative to the timestamp you are making a decision at. An as-of join that takes "the last value at or before t" is honest; one that takes "the nearest value" reaches forward by up to half a grid step and hands your backtest information it did not have. Interpolation is worse still — it is forward-looking by construction, and the resulting strategy will be profitable in every backtest and in no live session.
Part 4

4Five ways an O(1) claim turns out to be false

Every one of these passes its unit tests. Open them.

Recomputing the window because the code reads better

std::accumulate(w.begin(), w.end(), 0.0) / w.size() inside on_book. It is one line, it is obviously correct, and it costs O(k) per tick. At k = 4 096 and 200 000 messages a second you are asking for four hundred million loads a second for a number you could have maintained with an add and a subtract.

The trap is that it does not fail — it just consumes your budget, so every other decision you make gets less room, and the effect grows the moment you widen the window to smooth the signal.

Fix: keep a running sum and adjust it by the arriving and departing sample. If you need variance, use Welford. If you need min or max, use a monotonic deque — amortised O(1) and bounded. Then widening the window is free, which is the point.

Sum of squares minus square of sum

var = (sumsq - sum*sum/n) / (n-1) is algebraically correct and numerically hopeless. On a price series around 4 000 with a standard deviation of 0.02, both terms are enormous and nearly equal, so the subtraction cancels away almost every significant digit. You will see variances that are negative, which then produce a NaN standard deviation, which then silently poisons every signal downstream.

It is the classic case of a formula that is fine on paper and wrong in floating point, and it appears in production code constantly because it looks like the definition.

Fix: Welford. Same O(1) cost, same three scalars, updates the mean before using it in the M2 term so the differences stay small. Also clamp: a variance estimate below zero is a bug, not a value — assert it rather than sqrt it.

Reporting the mean when you are graded on the tail

Your handler averages 380 ns and you write "sub-microsecond" in the report. One tick in a thousand takes 40 µs because that is the tick where the vector reallocated, or the page was not resident, or the counter's ring wrapped into cold memory. Your mean is unchanged. Your p99.9 is a hundred times your median, and the trade you lost was in that tail.

Worse, tail events are not independent of what matters: reallocation happens when the message rate is high, and the message rate is high exactly when there is something to trade.

Fix: keep a histogram, not a running mean. Report p50 / p99 / p99.9 / max per stage. Widget 5 is the arithmetic for why the per-stage number is not the end-to-end number — measure both.

Assuming count() may be called with a clock that goes backwards

The RollingCounter contract says now_ns is non-decreasing, and that single guarantee is what makes the amortised O(1) argument work: an event expired once stays expired, so the head index only ever advances. Hand it a timestamp from a different clock — or from system_clock, which NTP can step backwards — and the head has already eaten samples that are live again. The count is now wrong and no loop will recover them.

The same failure sinks any monotonic-deque min/max: those structures discard information they can prove is dominated, and the proof depends on time moving one way.

Fix: take timestamps from std::chrono::steady_clock (or the NIC's hardware timestamp), never system_clock, on any path that feeds a windowed structure. Assert non-decreasing in a debug build so the violation surfaces where it happens.

Aligning two venues on your own receive timestamp

You join venue A and venue B on the time your process saw each message. Venue A is 40 µs closer to you than venue B, so every one of A's prints looks earlier than it was, and A appears to lead B — a beautiful, stable, entirely manufactured signal that will be arbitraged away the moment you colocate differently.

Receive time is a fact about your network, not about the market. Exchange send time is a fact about the market, subject to the exchanges' clocks agreeing, which is what the skew slider in Widget 6 is for.

Fix: carry both timestamps end to end and align on exchange time; use receive time only to measure your own latency. Measure the skew between venues explicitly and treat any apparent lead smaller than it as noise.
