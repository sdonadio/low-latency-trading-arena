HFT Landscape & Market Microstructure

High-frequency trading is not forecasting. It is a race: two bots running the same idea finish in the order their messages reach the matching engine. Before you write a line of C++ you need to know what you are racing for — a place in a queue — and what it is worth.

  • Read a central limit order book the way the matching engine reads it: two sorted sides, price first, then time.
  • Compute the four numbers your bot actually trades on — spread, mid, microprice, obi — and say which way each of them leans.
  • Explain, with a number, why a 10 µs bot beats a 100 µs bot even when both are right about the direction.
  • Get a C++ client onto the arena's LATENCY board and read your own p50 / p99 / p99.9.

Part 1

1What is it? Four participants and one data structure

Every modern equity, futures and crypto venue runs the same thing: a central limit order book — resting orders on two sides, matched by rules that fit on an index card. Everything else in this course is a consequence of those rules and of the fact that several people are trying to obey them faster than you.

      The four numbers, and what each one leans on

      TermDefinitionWhat it is for
      best bid / askhighest buy, lowest sell restingthe touch — where you can trade now
      spreadask − bidthe price of immediacy, in ticks
      mid(bid + ask) / 2a reference, not a prediction
      microprice(ask·bid_sz + bid·ask_sz) / (bid_sz + ask_sz)size-weighted touch — leans toward the thin side
      obi(bid_sz − ask_sz) / (bid_sz + ask_sz)imbalance in [−1, 1]; +1 = all bids
      depthresting quantity per levelhow far a market order walks
      queue_aheadsame-side, same-price qty in front of youhow much must trade before you fill
      Note the cross-weighting in the microprice. The bid size multiplies the ask price. With 800 lots bid and 200 offered, buyers dominate: obi = +0.6 and the microprice sits at 100.016 — above the 100.010 mid, leaning toward the ask. Over a short horizon the microprice predicts the next mid better than the mid does, and that lean is the cheapest alpha in the building.
      // book_metrics.cpp — real, compilable C++20
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstdio>

struct Metrics { double mid, spread, micro, obi; };

constexpr Metrics compute(double bid_px, double bid_sz,
                          double ask_px, double ask_sz) noexcept {
    const double sz = bid_sz + ask_sz;
    return Metrics{
        .mid    = 0.5 * (bid_px + ask_px),
        .spread = std::max(0.0, ask_px - bid_px),   // locked/crossed -> 0
        .micro  = sz > 0.0 ? (ask_px * bid_sz + bid_px * ask_sz) / sz
                           : 0.5 * (bid_px + ask_px),
        .obi    = sz > 0.0 ? (bid_sz - ask_sz) / sz : 0.0};
}

constexpr bool near(double a, double b, double eps = 1e-9) noexcept {
    return (a > b ? a - b : b - a) <= eps;
}

// the balanced-book sanity check, moved to compile time
static_assert(compute(100.00, 500, 100.02, 500).obi == 0.0);
// NOT ==: micro is 100.01000000000001, mid is 100.00999999999999
static_assert(near(compute(100.00, 500, 100.02, 500).micro,
                   compute(100.00, 500, 100.02, 500).mid));

int main() {
    const auto m = compute(100.00, 800, 100.02, 200);
    std::printf("mid=%.4f spread=%.4f micro=%.4f obi=%.4f\n",
                m.mid, m.spread, m.micro, m.obi);
    // mid=100.0100 spread=0.0200 micro=100.0160 obi=0.6000
}

      clang++ -std=c++20 -O2 book_metrics.cpp — and note the second static_assert. Writing it as micro == mid does not compile: the two expressions differ by one ulp (100.01000000000001 against 100.00999999999999) because they sum the same terms in a different order. Two computed doubles are never compared with ==. Week 6 is about moving much more of your checking here, to compile time.

      The two rules, and nothing else. Price first — a more aggressive price always trades ahead of a worse one. Then time — at the same price, whoever arrived earlier fills first, strictly FIFO. That is the entire matching algorithm, and it is why speed is worth money: it is the only way to buy the second rule.
      Part 3

      3Why it matters: 10 µs versus 100 µs, in fills

      Here is the payoff, and it is arithmetic, not attitude. You and a rival see the same book update at the same instant and both decide to post the same limit order at the same price. The engine does not know who is cleverer. It timestamps arrivals. The slower order sits behind the faster one, and when a taker arrives with less quantity than the queue, only the front of the queue gets paid.

      What the board would say

      The mean is not on the board. Turn on one malloc per tick: the median barely moves and p99.9 explodes, because an allocator call is usually a few tens of nanoseconds and occasionally a lock, a free-list walk or a page fault. Weeks 3 and 4 exist to delete that line from your hot path; Week 14 teaches you to prove it is gone.
      // include/hft_bot.hpp — the one hook that matters
void on_book(const std::string& symbol,
             double bid, double ask, double mid,
             double microprice, double obi) override {
    if (bid <= 0.0 || ask <= 0.0) return;      // need two sides
    if (ask - bid < 0.02) return;              // spread too tight
    // orders sent from here are latency-stamped for you
    if (obi > 0.4) buy_limit(symbol, 1, bid);
}
      Part 4

      4Five ways week one goes wrong

      These are the mistakes that survive all fifteen weeks if nobody names them now. Open them.

      Treating the mid as fair value

      The mid is the average of two prices and ignores how much size stands behind each one. With 100.00 × 800 bid and 100.02 × 200 offered, the mid says 100.010 and the book says buyers are three times as eager: obi = +0.6, microprice 100.016. A strategy that mean-reverts to the mid here is systematically selling into pressure.

      Fix: use the microprice as your reference and the mid only as a display number. Both arrive pre-computed in on_book, so there is no excuse of cost — and Week 8 shows you how to update them in O(1) when you compute them yourself.

      Repricing on every tick, and never counting the queue you threw away

      Cancel plus re-post is two cheap messages and one expensive consequence: a new timestamp, which means the back of the queue. If 900 lots were ahead of you and you were 200 from the front, one reprice can cost you the whole session's fills at that level.

      The tell is a bot with an excellent order-to-trade ratio in the wrong direction: thousands of messages, almost no fills, and a P&L that is entirely fees.

      Fix: put a hysteresis band on requoting — only move when the microprice has left the band by more than the queue is worth — and track queue_ahead from the acknowledgement so the cost is a number in your log, not a feeling.

      Assuming a fill is good news

      Your resting bid fills instantly and in full. That usually means the market just decided your price was generous: somebody with better information crossed the spread to hit you. This is adverse selection, and it is why the fastest passive fills are often the worst ones.

      Fix: measure a markout — the mid 1 s, 5 s and 30 s after each fill — and grade yourself on markout-adjusted P&L, not on fill count. Speed's second job, after winning the queue, is letting you cancel before the bad fill happens.

      Optimising the mean because the mean is what your printf shows

      A 38 µs average with a 5.2 ms worst tick is a losing bot, and the average will never tell you. The races that pay are concentrated in the busiest microseconds of the session — exactly when queues are longest, when everyone fires at once, and when your tail shows up.

      Fix: collect a sample, sort it, and report p50 / p99 / p99.9 from the first week. The arena's harness already does; make your own benchmarks match it so the numbers are comparable.

      Measuring latency with the wrong clock — or with the print inside the timer

      std::chrono::system_clock is wall-clock: it can step backwards when NTP corrects it, which turns a percentile table into fiction. And a std::cout inside the timed region measures your terminal, not your strategy — a flush is easily tens of microseconds.

      Fix: std::chrono::steady_clock (monotonic) around the smallest region you mean to measure; log to a pre-allocated ring buffer and drain it off the hot path. Week 2 builds the harness properly, and Week 14 shows what the harness still hides.
