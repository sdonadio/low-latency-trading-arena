Profiling, the Tail & Production

You have the platform. Now prove it is fast — not fast on average. A 40 µs mean hides the 5 ms tick that lost the race, and every tail spike has a physical cause with a signature you can learn. This week you stop guessing: measure, attribute, fix, re-measure, and ship something that survives contact with a live session.

  • Benchmark without lying to yourself: warm-up, repeats, min vs mean vs median, and what a laptop adds to every number you take on it.
  • Read a flame graph of a tick-to-trade path and tell self time from total time.
  • Explain why a 7-stage pipeline that is fast 99% of the time per stage is fast only 93% of the time end to end.
  • Name the tail source from the histogram it produces — fault, allocation, preemption, interrupt, thermal — and kill it.
  • Use markouts as a production metric: a fast fill can be a bad fill, and you can prove which.

Part 1

1What is it? Measurement, before anything else

Latency distributions are not Gaussian. They are heavy-tailed and usually bimodal: a tight fast body, and a sparse population of rare catastrophic stalls. The mean sits in the valley between them, describing a tick that nobody ever experiences. Percentiles are not a stylistic preference in this course — they are the only honest summary of a shape like that.

The shape, drawn honestly

        fast body            tail
  count |####                .
        |#######             .
        |##########          .  ← p99.9
        |#########           .     lives
        |####            .   .     HERE
        +--------------------------→ µs
         mean = 40    p99.9 = 210   max = 5200

Report p50, p99, p99.9 and the max, per window. A p99 computed over a whole session hides two minutes where it was the p50 — and the max is the number your risk case has to survive.

Coordinated omission

The subtlest way to under-report a tail. If you time request to request — start the clock when the previous one finished — then a stall that delays ten requests is recorded once, and the nine it pushed back are simply never sampled. Your histogram loses exactly the events that hurt.

Measure on a fixed schedule: the tick was supposed to be handled at T, so record now − T, including the time you spent not being able to start. That is the number the market saw.

In the arena. The exchange stamps you from the book_snapshot it sent to the order you sent back — a fixed origin it controls, not one you choose. You cannot omit your way to a good score.

The harness that does not lie

using clk = std::chrono::steady_clock;

// stop the optimizer deleting the work you are timing
template <class T>
inline void do_not_optimize(T& v) {
  asm volatile("" : "+r,m"(v) : : "memory");
}

for (std::size_t i = 0; i < WARMUP; ++i)
  sink += roll.push(px[i]);          // untimed

for (std::size_t i = 0; i < N; ++i) {
  const auto t0 = clk::now();
  sink += roll.push(px[WARMUP + i]);  // the hot path
  const auto t1 = clk::now();
  do_not_optimize(sink);
  lat_ns.push_back(
    std::chrono::duration<double, std::nano>(t1 - t0).count());
}
Part 2

2How it works — perf, the flame graph, and where the tail comes from

Three verbs, in order, every time: perf stat for the totals, perf record for the stacks, perf report or a flame graph to read them. Intuition about hotspots is wrong more often than it is right, and it is wrong most confidently on the tail.

The three verbs, cold

# 1. cheap summary counters — always start here
perf stat -d ./build/hft_bot --replay tape.bin

# 2. sample the call stacks at 4 kHz with DWARF unwinding
perf record -F 4000 -g --call-graph dwarf \
     ./build/hft_bot --replay tape.bin

# 3. read where the cycles went
perf report --stdio | head -40
perf annotate on_book        # cycles per source line

# and the picture: fold the stacks into a flame graph
perf script | stackcollapse-perf.pl | flamegraph.pl > tail.svg

Counters tell you why a frame is hot

  • cache-misses, LLC-load-misses — a DRAM trip is 200+ cycles. Usually the real cost behind a "slow line".
  • branch-misses — a flush is ~15–20 cycles. A hot unpredictable branch is a candidate to make branchless (week 13).
  • page-faults — should be zero after startup on a hot path. Any nonzero count is a tail source you can name.
  • context-switches, cpu-migrations — should also be zero. If they are not, you did not really get the core.
  • IPC — instructions per cycle. Low IPC with high cycles means stalling, not computing.
Keep -g in the release build. Debug symbols cost nothing at run time and are the difference between a readable flame graph and a wall of hex addresses. Optimize and keep symbols.

Interactive · a flame graph of the tick-to-trade path

Width is time. Click a frame — or tab to it and press Enter — to see its total time (the frame plus everything it called) against its self time (the frame alone). Wide plateaus are the targets; tall thin spikes are just deep call stacks and cost nothing.

How to read it

  • Total is why a frame is on the graph. Self is why it is your problem.
  • A frame with 60% total and 2% self is not slow — its children are. Go down.
  • Allocator frames under a function that "just averages numbers" are the smoking gun.
  • Sampling is statistical: a frame that appears in 3 samples out of 4000 is noise, not a finding.

Interactive · why the tail is the product

A tick-to-trade path is a chain, and a chain is only as fast as its slowest link on that tick. If each of N stages is "fine" 99% of the time, the odds that all of them are fine is 0.99N — which falls off a cliff faster than anyone's intuition.

Interactive · switch the tail sources on and off

There is no garbage collector in C++, and it does not matter: five other mechanisms produce the same shape. Tick a source and watch it appear in the histogram — each one has its own signature, which is how you recognise it in a real session.

The homework, in two files

starters/hw14/tail.cpp processes two million synthetic ticks and prints the distribution. Its median is fine; its tail is not. The pathology is on the hot path and there are two of them stacked — a heap allocation and an O(window) recompute.

Before — the shipped starter

// a fresh vector per tick: malloc + push_back growth
static double signal(const std::vector<double>& prices,
                     std::size_t i, std::size_t window) {
  std::size_t lo = i >= window ? i - window : 0;
  std::vector<double> scratch;        // (a) allocates
  for (std::size_t k = lo; k <= i; ++k)
    scratch.push_back(prices[k]);
  double s = 0.0;
  for (double v : scratch) s += v;  // (b) O(window)
  return s / scratch.size();
}

Measured here with clang++ -O2 -std=c++17 on an ordinary laptop: p50 458 ns · p99 791 ns · p99.9 1333 ns · max 178 µs. The median is unremarkable and the max is 390× it.

After — one buffer, one add, one subtract

// O(1) per tick, zero allocation on the hot path
struct RollingMean {
  std::vector<double> buf;   // reserved once, used as a ring
  std::size_t window, count = 0, head = 0;
  double sum = 0.0;

  explicit RollingMean(std::size_t w) : buf(w, 0.0), window(w) {}

  double push(double px) {
    if (count == window) sum -= buf[head];  // evict oldest
    else                 ++count;
    sum += px;                              // admit newest
    buf[head] = px;
    head = (head + 1 == window) ? 0 : head + 1;
    return sum / static_cast<double>(count);
  }
};

Same machine, same flags: p50 41 ns · p99 42 ns · p99.9 42 ns. The body got 11× faster and the tail collapsed onto the median — which is the actual deliverable.

The trap in the fix. The original window is inclusive of i and grows from 1 to window at the start. If your warm-up does not ramp count the same way, the sink changes — and a faster program that computes something else is not a fix. Match the warm-up, then diff the sink.
Part 3

3Why it matters — the tail is the grade, and markouts are the truth

Two production metrics decide whether a fast bot is a good bot. The first is the tail, because the board sorts on it. The second is the markout: what the mid did after your fill. A bot with a beautiful p99.9 and a negative markout is not winning — it is being run over quickly.

Interactive · the markout curve

A markout marks each fill against the reference mid some time later: markout = sign(side) × (mid_later − mid_at_fill) × qty. Positive means the market came to you; negative means you were picked off. Choose a cohort of fills and watch the curve develop across the horizons the arena computes.

—

The metric, as the server computes it

// per fill, at each horizon: +100 ms, +1 s, +5 s
double markout(int side, double fill_px,
               double mid_later, double qty) {
  const double s = (side == BUY) ? 1.0 : -1.0;
  return s * (mid_later - fill_px) * qty;
}
// negative and persistent on one symbol or one
// counterparty => toxic flow: widen, skew away, or pull.

Production practice — what "ready" means

  • Kill switch. One flag, checked on the hot path, that stops all quoting and cancels everything resting. It must work when the strategy thread is the thing that is broken.
  • Hard limits, checked before send. Position, notional, order rate, max order size, price sanity band. A limit you check after the send is a post-mortem, not a control.
  • Deterministic replay. One tape in, one identical order sequence out. If a run is not reproducible you cannot bisect a regression, and you cannot prove a fix.
  • Canary before fleet. Ship the new binary to one instance on a small size, compare its tail and its markout against the incumbent, then promote. "It built" is not a deployment plan.
  • Logging off the hot path. The hot thread pushes a fixed-size binary record into a lock-free SPSC ring; a reader thread formats and writes it. A synchronous write or a string format on the hot path is a guaranteed tail event.
  • Counters, not printfs. Atomic counters for fills, rejects, requotes, faults — scraped from the reader thread, plotted live. A rising p99.9 is an early warning; nobody reads a log fast enough to be one.
Sanitizers are a different build. ASan + UBSan in one, TSan in another, both in CI, both on the replay — and never shipped: 2–20× slower. The graded binary is -O3 -march=native -flto -DNDEBUG -g.

Part 4

4Five ways this goes wrong

Four of these produce a number that looks fine and is wrong. The fifth produces a number that is right and useless.

Benchmarking code the compiler already deleted

You time a pure function, never use the result, and the optimizer — correctly — removes the call. Now you are timing two clock::now() calls and reporting 0.4 ns as the cost of your signal. It is the fastest wrong answer in this course, and it gets submitted every year.

The same failure with a subtler face: hoisting. A loop-invariant computation gets lifted out of your timing loop, so you measure it once and divide by a thousand.

Fix: accumulate into a sink you print at the end, and put a do_not_optimize barrier around it. Then sanity-check the magnitude — if a function that touches 256 doubles claims 1 ns, it did not run.

Reporting the mean, or a percentile over the wrong window

A 40 µs mean over a bimodal distribution describes a tick that never happened. And a p99 over a whole session hides a two-minute stretch where it was the p50 — the session-wide number is an average of percentiles, which is not a percentile of anything.

The mirror error is reporting only the min because "that is what the code can do". The market does not give you your best case; it gives you the case you got.

Fix: keep the raw samples. Report p50, p99, p99.9 and the max, per window, with the sample count next to them. Plot the histogram — and if you only ever look at one number, look at p99.9.

Timing request-to-request (coordinated omission)

Start the clock when the last tick finished, and a 5 ms stall is recorded exactly once — while the ten ticks it delayed are never sampled at all, because you were not there to sample them. Your histogram is a record of the moments the system was healthy.

This is why load generators that back off under stress systematically under-report tails, and why a "p99.9 of 60 µs" from such a harness can coexist with visibly missed races.

Fix: measure against a fixed schedule or an externally stamped origin — for a tick that arrived at T, record now − T. In the arena the exchange owns that origin, which is the whole point of it owning the stamp.

Optimizing what the profiler did not point at

A frame with 60% total time and 2% self time is not slow; its children are. Rewriting it buys nothing. Equally, a tall narrow tower in a flame graph is a deep call stack, not a hot one — width is time, height is only depth.

And a sampling profiler is statistical: a symbol with 3 samples out of 4000 is noise. Chasing it is how an afternoon disappears.

Fix: read self versus total before you touch anything, sample long enough that the hot frames have hundreds of samples, and re-run perf stat after the change. If the counters did not move, neither did your latency.

A perfect p99.9 with a negative markout

This is the one that is technically correct and commercially useless. You made the bot fast, so it now gets filled instantly — and it gets filled instantly precisely when someone better informed wants the other side. Fast fills are selected fills.

It shows up as a healthy fill rate, a healthy latency board position, and a markout at +1 s that is consistently negative on one symbol or against one counterparty. The rebate hides it in the raw P&L, which is exactly why the arena computes markout separately.

Fix: measure markout per cohort — passive versus aggressive, per symbol, per counterparty where you can see it. Where it is persistently negative, widen, skew away, or stop quoting that name. Being fast is a licence to be selective, not a reason to trade more.

Part 6

6Do the work

In the lab (~45–60 min)

  • Build and run starters/hw14/tail.cpp at -O2 — the point is to profile the algorithm, not a missing optimizer. Run it several times and watch p50 hold still while p99.9 and max jump around.
  • Form the hypothesis from the source before you profile: a per-tick allocation, and an O(window) recompute.
  • Prove it with perf record -g → perf report, then fold the stacks into a flame graph. No perf? valgrind --tool=callgrind, Instruments, or even ltrace -c to count the malloc calls — the attribution is the deliverable, not the tool.
  • Fix it in tail_fixed.cpp: one reserved ring, one running sum. Match the warm-up so the sink is unchanged, then quote the before/after ratio.
  • Wire up the sanitizers on a debug build and fix anything they flag.

Homework 14 & the report

  • HW14: tail_fixed.cpp with an identical sink, plus p50/p99/p99.9 before and after.
  • HW14: a one-page changelog naming the cause — not "made it faster" but "removed a per-tick heap allocation; the allocator's slow path was the p99.9".
  • In your bot: a flame graph of the on_book path, the top tail cause found and killed, and a measured p99.9 improvement from the harness on the same tape.
  • Ship: the graded release build, clean under ASan/UBSan and TSan.
  • Due week 15: the short profiling report — flame graph, the cause, the fix, the before/after tail histogram.
